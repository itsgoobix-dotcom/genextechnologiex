How Enzymes Work

BeginnerLevel
12 minDuration
4Concepts
Section 1

What is an enzyme?

An enzyme is a biological catalyst — usually a protein — that speeds up a specific chemical reaction inside a cell. Enzymes aren't consumed by the reactions they help along; they bind, assist, and release, ready to repeat the process again.

Almost every reaction that keeps you alive, from digesting food to copying DNA, relies on an enzyme to make it happen fast enough to matter.

Section 2

Activation energy

Every chemical reaction needs a small push of energy to get started — this is called activation energy. Without help, many biologically important reactions would happen far too slowly to sustain life.

Enzymes work by lowering this energy barrier, making it much easier for a reaction to proceed at the temperatures found inside a living cell.

Section 3

The active site

Every enzyme has an active site — a precisely shaped pocket where its target molecule, called the substrate, fits. The shape of the active site is specific, similar to how a lock accepts only a matching key.

This specificity is why a given enzyme usually acts on only one substrate, or a small family of closely related ones.

Section 4

The enzyme–substrate complex

When a substrate binds to the active site, the two form an enzyme–substrate complex. This brief, temporary structure positions the substrate exactly where the reaction needs to happen.

Binding can also slightly reshape the enzyme, a phenomenon called induced fit, which helps stress the substrate's bonds in just the right way.

Section 5

How catalysis works

Inside the complex, the enzyme stabilizes the substrate in a strained, transition state — the point of highest energy along the reaction. By stabilizing this state, the enzyme dramatically lowers the energy needed for the reaction to complete.

The reaction then proceeds far faster than it would without the enzyme's help — often by a factor of millions.

Section 6

Products are released

Once the reaction completes, the resulting products no longer fit the active site as well as the original substrate did. They're released, and the enzyme returns to its original shape — free to bind a new substrate and repeat the cycle.
